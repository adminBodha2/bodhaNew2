## About

Three contemporary scholars — one woman, two men — sit on the broad outer steps of a vast ancient temple, laptops and books in their hands. Behind them, carved into the temple's exterior walls in high relief, generations of scholars and sages are rendered in stone: figures in debate, rishis in teaching posture, commentators bent over texts. The stone figures and the living ones occupy the same frame. Below the steps, a vast open landscape extends into the distance. The living scholars appear small against the history of what they are continuing.

## Academy

A young scholar kneels before an aged guru in a stone courtyard attached to a temple — the traditional posture of first receiving knowledge. Behind the guru, visible in deepening distance, stands another guru teaching another student in the same posture, and behind that pair another, and further still another. Each pair is set in a progressively older era: contemporary dress giving way to colonial-era clothing, then classical Indian robes, then ancient forest garments. The nearest pair is in the present. The chain stretches back beyond the edge of what can be seen.

## Anveshi

A solitary yatri stands at the threshold of a great temple gopuram — the ornate gateway towering above him, its sculptural tiers rising into a sky filled with stars and planetary bodies. Through the gateway, a path extends that leads simultaneously inward toward the temple's innermost sanctum and outward into open cosmic space, as if the sacred site is both a physical destination and a departure point into something larger than itself. The figure stands at the threshold with one foot still on the approach path, not yet having crossed.

## Anveshi — Badami Chapter

Inside a rock-cut cave temple carved from red sandstone — the rear wall occupied by a monumental relief of Vishnu in the Trivikrama form, one foot pressed to the earth, one leg raised in a stride that spans the three worlds. The carved figure fills the full height of the cave wall. A group of contemporary visitors stands before it looking upward, their human scale making the deity's cosmic scale explicit. Through the cave's open entrance, the sandstone cliff continues upward into a sky where a spiral galaxy echoes the proportions of the cosmic figure carved within.

## Anveshi — Gujarat Chapter

A great ancient stepped well viewed from directly above, looking straight down into its depths. The geometry is exact — symmetrical tiers of carved stone steps descending in perfect order, columns and sculptural figures at every level, the architecture narrowing as the eye moves downward. At the very bottom, still water reflects the entire structure in reverse — and in that reflection, a sky is visible, and in the reflected sky, stars appear that are not present in the actual sky above. The well descends into the earth and opens simultaneously into the cosmos.

The temple walls, the night sky, and the flame form a single vertical axis from the earth to the cosmos.

## Anveshi — Kullu Chapter

The peaks in the background rise beyond their natural height, extending upward into a sky where stars are visible in daylight, as if the valley is not only a geographical place but a point where the sacred landscape extends into cosmic space. The palanquin is the center of the composition and of the world it moves through. The deity inside is present though not visible.

## Aryavarta (Scrolls of Aryavarta)

A massive illustrated manuscript lies open on a stone surface — its panels dense with scenes from Indian history: cavalry charges, temples in flame, prisoners in chains, a civilization under conquest. The figures in the illustrations are not contained by the page: a warrior's arm extends beyond the panel border into the surrounding margin, a horse's hooves press into the stone surface below the manuscript. The record refuses to remain flat. At the far edges of the outermost panels, barely present, the same temples and the same landscape appear again — still standing, still there.

## Big Questions

A single human figure stands at the center of a vast stone courtyard — enclosed on three sides by ancient carved walls, every surface dense with inscribed text and sculptural relief, questions and responses layered over each other across centuries. Above the courtyard, the ceiling is the cosmos: stars and galaxies pressing downward into the open space. The fourth wall is absent; where it should be, open sky continues unbroken into the cosmic space above. The figure stands at the exact meeting point of the architectural enclosure and the infinite opening, facing neither the inscribed walls nor the open sky, simply present at the threshold.

## Big Question — What Is the Core of Sanatana Dharma

A single oil lamp burns at the center of a vast empty stone floor — a temple sanctuary, the space extending in all directions. The flame is small and steady. From it, rings of illumination expand outward at successive distances to reveal different forms: closest, a yantra drawn in rice flour; further, a bronze Nataraja in the dance pose; further still, the carved base of a great lingam; and at the limit of what the flame can reach, the threshold stone of a temple entrance too large to be fully seen. Each form at each distance makes a different claim about where the center actually is. The flame is equidistant from all of them.

## Big Question — What Does Being Indian Mean

The Indian subcontinent rendered as a sacred geography — not a political map but a landscape of rivers and sacred sites. The Ganga, Yamuna, and Saraswati descend from Himalayan peaks in the north and flow outward toward the four sacred dhamas at the cardinal points of the landmass: Badrinath, Dwarka, Rameswaram, Puri. From each sacred site, threads of connection radiate outward toward all the others, making the subcontinent a web of living relationships. Three figures stand at different points on this geography — one at the center where rivers converge, one at the northwestern edge where the land meets contested territory, one at the southern coast facing the ocean. Each occupies the same sacred map and understands their belonging through it entirely differently.

## Big Question — What Will Destroy Hindu Society

An ancient Hindu temple stands complete and majestic in a vast open landscape, its shikhara rising against a cosmic sky. Multiple forces press against it simultaneously from different directions: a tide of blank modern structures encroaching from one side; the architecture of missionary activity visible at another angle — a cross lit against the dark sky; and within the temple itself, hairline cracks in the stone where tradition is forgetting its own interior life. The temple still stands. Its flame still burns inside the sanctum. But the pressures are real, multiple, and closing in from every direction at once.

## Big Question — Is Modernity Compatible With Hindu Values

A Vastu Purusha Mandala — the ancient cosmic grid used as the proportional foundation for sacred architecture — drawn on stone with great precision, the Purusha figure at its center. Overlaid on the same surface, in a different hand, a modern architectural blueprint: rooms, structural columns, circulation corridors. The two grids share the same floor without merging, each drawn over the other without erasing the other. A pair of hands holds both drawings simultaneously, studying where the two systems of proportion coincide and where they diverge.

## Big Question — Is Hindu Unity Compatible With Indian Diversity

A vast river system viewed from high above — the rivers of the subcontinent visible as an interconnected network flowing from Himalayan sources downward. At the top of the frame the rivers are separate, each flowing from its own source, their individual characters distinct. Moving downward through the frame they join, diverge, and rejoin. At the bottom of the frame, where the rivers reach the ocean, the waters are indistinguishable. ## Big Question — Does India Need Another Republic

Moving inward through successive rings of columns, each layer holds the instruments of an older political tradition: medieval administration, classical polity, ancient dharmashastra. At the innermost chamber — the garbha griha of this political architecture — a single flame burns on a stone altar. The hall is active and inhabited at every ring simultaneously. No ring is empty. No ring agrees with the others about which one is the center.

## Big Question — What Idea Must Die for Hindu Renaissance

A single great crack runs through the wall from its base to its top. Through the widening gap on the far side of the wall, an interior is visible: a vast and active temple — fully intact, its rituals in progress, its traditions alive — exactly the civilization the wall's inscriptions claim does not exist. ## Big Question — What Do Hindus Practice But Cannot Prove

A figure sits in deep meditation on a stone floor — cross-legged, entirely still, eyes closed. Above the meditating figure, the upper half of the frame opens into the full depth of the cosmos: galaxies, nebulae, the structure of deep space. Between the seated body and the cosmic space, a transparent boundary — like the surface of a vast sphere — passes at the level of the figure's crown. The body is below it, physical and present. The consciousness has passed through. No instrument in the lower half of the frame reaches through the boundary. The passage is real. So is the boundary.

The ceiling of the hall is transparent, and through it the night sky is visible in full depth: stars, galaxies, the deep universe pressing down into the hall. From the figure at the podium, a line of light rises through the transparent ceiling and connects to the cosmic space above, joining the moment in the hall to an ancient and vast source.

## Research

Above the courtyard, the same scene is repeated at escalating scales visible simultaneously: the village within the district, the district within the state, the state within the subcontinent — each scale layered upward in the sky. At the center of every scale, the ethnographer's pen touches the page. What he records in the village ripples upward through every scale of the civilization above him.

Overlaid on exactly the same surface, in a different hand, is the governance chart of a living institution — its circles of function, its lines of accountability, its concentric rings of decision-making. The two structures use identical proportions. A scholar's hand traces a line where the two maps coincide, where the sacred and the institutional inhabit the same geometry.

Above the sanctum, through an opening in the stone ceiling, the cosmic sky is visible — and from that vantage, the entire composition of rings around the central flame is legible as a living mandala, the same geometric principle as the sacred architecture made alive in human activity.

## Research — Structure of Hindu Charity

The act of feeding is connected by an unbroken thread to a principle as large as the universe. No one in the kitchen is looking upward. The fire connects them anyway.

## Research — Temples of Madhya Pradesh

## Research — Battle for Dharma in North East

A hillside village in Assam — bamboo homes on the slope, rice terraces below, forest above. At one end of the village, a traditional Vaishnava namghar with a pitched bamboo roof. Fifty meters away, a church with a tin roof and a painted cross. Between the two structures, daily village life continues: figures moving, cooking fires burning, children playing in the central space. In the sky above, the same confrontation is mirrored at cosmic scale: two vast civilizational forces face each other across the sky as they face each other across the village clearing. The figures in the village are small. What they are caught between is enormous.

## Research — Hindu Aesthetics and Purva Paksha of Western Art

A great Nataraja — Shiva in the cosmic dance — occupies the center of the frame, the ring of fire encircling the figure, the cosmic proportion of the form exact. Around the Nataraja at increasing distances, observers gather: on one side, Western art critics with open books, their annotations visible — "lacks anatomical proportion," "without chronological development"; on the other side, scholars of the Hindu aesthetic tradition with the Natyashastra and Shilpa Shastra open, their annotations mapping the cosmic iconometry that governs every measurement of the form. The Nataraja does not address any of them. It dances. The same figure is seen as two entirely different things from either side.

## Research — Hindu Response to Enlightenment

Two vast civilizational maps face each other across an open cosmic space. One shows the cosmology of Western modernity: reason, the autonomous individual, the nation-state, linear progress, empirical measurement as the limit of knowledge. The other shows the philosophical cosmology of the Hindu tradition: consciousness as primary reality, dharmic order, cyclical time, the Purusha as the measure of all things. Between the two maps, where they almost touch, two contemporary figures sit across a table — one from each tradition, both mid-conversation, their texts open and heavily annotated on both sides. The maps behind them are cosmic in scale. The conversation between them is close and specific.

## Research — Hindu School of History

A historian stands between two enormous diagrams suspended in the space of a vast library. On one side: the Western periodization of Indian history — a horizontal timeline moving from primitive to medieval to colonial to modern, its arrows pointing in a single direction, its categories imposed from outside. On the other side: a Hindu visualization of time — a spiral rather than a line, its cycles marked in yugas, pilgrimage networks radiating outward from sacred centers, oral tradition shown as primary evidence, the Puranas and Itihasas present as historical sources. The historian is a small figure between the two great structures, one hand touching each. Before him, a blank paper.

## Wiki

Indra's Net — the infinite web of mutually reflecting jewels — rendered as a specific intellectual geography. Each jewel is a node in a knowledge network: the face of a thinker, the text of a question, the image of a sacred site, a concept in Sanskrit. Each jewel reflects every other jewel in its surface — the philosopher visible in the jewel of the temple, the temple visible in the jewel of the question, the question visible in the philosopher. The net extends in all directions without a visible center or boundary. At one point in the net, a pair of human hands reaches in and takes hold of one jewel — and the entire net moves gently in response to that single point of contact.

## Bodha (Key Image)

The Ashvattha — the sacred tree of Hindu cosmology, whose roots are above and whose branches grow downward — fills the frame from top to bottom. The roots at the top of the frame reach into cosmic space: galaxies, the deep structure of the universe, the source of being. The branches descend downward through the frame and grow progressively more concrete: Vedic hymns in the highest branches, Upanishadic philosophy in the middle, classical temple architecture and the tradition's living forms further down, contemporary scholarship and research at the lowest branches where human figures work among the foliage. The tree grows downward from its cosmic source. The scholars at the ends of the lowest branches are connected, through the entire structure of the tree, to what is above.
