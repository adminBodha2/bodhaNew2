Two people encounter the same poem. One is moved to rasa. The other shrugs. Where does the difference live?

The tradition's answer is structural: the poem alone is not sufficient. The aesthetic event requires both the work and a receiver constituted to receive it. The constituted receiver is the *sahridaya* - literally "same-hearted," the one whose cognitive and affective equipment is structured to resonate with what the work is doing.

*Sa-hridaya*: having the heart with. The receiver's heart is aligned with the artwork's frequency. This alignment is not natural endowment; it is cultivated. The sahridaya has been shaped through repeated exposure to artworks, through the development of taste, through familiarity with the conventions and emotional registers the tradition works in. Anandavardhana and Abhinavagupta are explicit: without the sahridaya, the poem's dhvani (A1) does not resonate, the rasa (A2) does not arise, the artwork's structural conditions have nothing to bear on.

The design consequence is structural. An artist working seriously does not write for "the audience" in the generic sense - the artist writes for the sahridaya. The standard objection - but this leaves most people out - was anticipated and answered. The sahridaya is a constituted role; receivers can be cultivated into it through education, exposure, and practice. The artwork's effects scale with the receiver's qualification: a more fully constituted sahridaya receives more of what the work is doing, but partial qualification still receives partial effect. The standard is not exclusion. It is recognition that effect depends on capacity, and capacity is variable and cultivable.

The artwork-receiver pair is the unit of analysis. The artwork alone is half of it.

## Where English Falls Short

English has "audience" and "reader," both of which collapse the cultivation dimension. An audience is whoever is present; a reader is whoever reads. Sahridaya is whoever has been constituted to receive what this kind of work does. The English vocabulary's failure to mark this produces a recurring conflation: accessibility (more people can engage at all) is confused with qualification (some people can engage more deeply). These are different achievements with different implications for how a work should be built.

"Target audience" in marketing has the design-constraint direction without the cultivation dimension. A target audience is defined by demographics or psychographics; a sahridaya is defined by acquired capacity. The two come apart in practice. A demographically off-target receiver may be a sahridaya by cultivated taste; a demographically on-target receiver may lack the constitution to receive what the work is actually doing.

"User" in product work has its own version of the problem - it marks whoever uses, without distinguishing the user whose engagement is shallow from the one whose engagement is deep. The sahridaya frame asks what cultivation the deep engagement requires and whether the product is doing anything to develop it in users who do not yet have it.

The nearest critical-theory analog is the "implied reader" or "ideal reader." The apparatus is thinner. The Indian tradition adds the cultivation dimension (the sahridaya is made, not found), the affective dimension (heart-alignment, beyond cognitive competence), and the structural centrality of the receiver's constitution to whether the aesthetic effect occurs at all.

## Where it Shows Up

**Audience definition in product and content design.** "Who is this for?" is an underdeveloped version of the sahridaya question. The fuller version: what cultivation does the intended receiver have? What would they need in order for the design to deliver its intended effect? Is the design doing anything to develop that cultivation in those who do not yet have it? Most design briefs stop at the demographic.

**The problem of communicating expertise.** A specialist explaining their domain to a non-specialist faces a sahridaya gap. The explanation that works for the constituted receiver fails for the unconstituted. The traditional response - simplify - often abandons what was worth communicating. The sahridaya frame asks what minimum cultivation has to be supplied, and whether the explanation can supply it in motion.

**Onboarding and progressive disclosure.** A product that requires a sahridaya to be appreciated must either constitute the sahridaya during onboarding or accept that initial users will not see what advanced users see. Progressive disclosure is a partial answer. Cultivation is the deeper one.

**Critic and reviewer dynamics.** A reviewer constituted for one kind of work and evaluating another will systematically misread its structural intention. The sahridaya frame gives the structural account of why some critics are good readers of some works and not others - a structural fact the reviewing apparatus tends to ignore entirely.

**AI evaluation by humans.** When humans evaluate AI outputs, the evaluation depends on the evaluator's sahridaya constitution with respect to what the model is doing. Crowdworker evaluation produces one kind of judgement; expert evaluation produces another. The difference is not effort but constitution. Designing evaluation pipelines well requires matching the sahridaya to what is being evaluated.

**Brand and taste-community formation.** A brand cultivates a sahridaya community around itself. The community develops the taste to appreciate what the brand is doing; the brand's products become legible at a level invisible to outsiders. The cultivation is the brand-building work that the sahridaya concept names structurally.

**Writing for someone in particular.** The practical advice to write for one specific reader is the sahridaya principle in practice. The one reader is a constituted sahridaya; writing aimed at them lands more cleanly with everyone close to them in constitution than writing aimed at "the audience" ever would.

## IKS Roots

The Sanskrit term is *sahridaya* (सहृदय), from *saha* (with, together) + *hridaya* (heart): "having heart with," the one whose heart is aligned with the artwork's. The technical use runs through Anandavardhana's *Dhvanyaloka* (9th century) and is elaborated by Abhinavagupta in the *Locana* (commentary on the *Dhvanyaloka*) and the *Abhinavabharati* (commentary on the *Natyashastra*). Abhinavagupta's account is rich: the sahridaya is one whose mind has been polished by repeated exposure to good poetry, whose imagination is active, whose taste has been refined, and whose affective channels are open enough for the artwork's structural conditions to produce rasa. The cognate term *rasika* (the one who tastes rasa) appears interchangeably in much of the literature. The concept is not Anandavardhana's invention - Bharata's *Natyashastra* already presupposes a qualified audience - but the explicit treatment of the sahridaya as a structural design constraint is the Kashmiri dhvani tradition's contribution. The doctrine carries consequences for how the tradition thinks about literary education, the role of the commentator (who helps the partially qualified reader become more fully sahridaya), and the social conditions for the flourishing of poetry.

See also A1 (dhvani - the resonant meaning the sahridaya is constituted to receive), A2 (rasa - the aesthetic emotion that arises in the sahridaya), and A3 (vakrokti - the oblique expression whose camatkara requires the sahridaya to flash).
