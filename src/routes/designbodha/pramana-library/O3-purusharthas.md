The classical Indian tradition names four aims that together constitute a full human life. *Purushartha* - literally "the aims of the human." The four are dharma, artha, kama, and moksha. The list is older than systematic philosophy; it appears in the Epics, in Dharmashastra, in early ritual literature, and is treated as the basic frame within which other questions about how to live are even asked.

*Dharma* is the aim of right ordering - the structuring of life by what one's role, place, time, and constitution require. What does this situation call for from someone in my position, and am I meeting that call? Dharma includes ethical obligation, but the term is wider: it covers the ordering of action by what is appropriate, including obligations to oneself, to others, to institutions, to the structure of things. Dharma derives from the root *dhr* (to hold, to sustain) - that which sustains right order.

*Artha* is the aim of means - wealth, resources, power, security, the material and political conditions that enable other action. The tradition treats artha as a legitimate aim in its own right. Kautilya's *Arthashastra* (4th century BCE) is the systematic treatise on its disciplined pursuit, especially political and economic power. The aim has its own discipline, its own ethics, its own characteristic excellences.

*Kama* is the aim of desire and pleasure - the satisfaction of want, the experience of beauty, the enjoyment of relationship, the cultivation of sensuous and aesthetic life. The *Kamasutra* (3rd century CE), routinely reduced in Western imagination to its erotic content, is in actual structure a sophisticated treatise on the cultivation of pleasure as a structured life-aim: the household arts, the conduct of relationship, the connoisseurship of food and music and conversation, alongside the sexual content.

*Moksha* is the aim of liberation - the release from the structural constraints that the first three aims operate within. The recognition that the self is not exhausted by dharma, artha, and kama, and the pursuit of what lies beyond them. In Advaita Vedanta this becomes the recognition of the witness as brahman; in Yoga the cessation of vrittis (Y1); in Buddhism the cessation of suffering.

The four are constitutive, not alternatives. Each has its own legitimacy, its own discipline, its own pathology. Dharma without artha and kama becomes joyless rigidity. Artha without dharma becomes domination; without kama, miserly. Kama without dharma becomes destructive indulgence; without moksha, a closed loop. Moksha pursued without prior maturation in the other three becomes premature renunciation - usually a hidden form of escapism. The tradition is clear on this.

## Where English Falls Short

Western value frameworks reduce to single axes. Utilitarianism reduces to pleasure/happiness - the kama axis, hypertrophied. Deontology reduces to obligation - the dharma axis, isolated. Virtue ethics reduces to character - a partial reading of dharma. Liberalism reduces to autonomous choice - a procedural frame that doesn't name the aims of life at all. Marxism reduces to material conditions - the artha axis, theologised. Each names a real dimension of human life. Each becomes pathological when treated as the whole.

The purusharthas refuse the reduction. They hold the four aims in plural and insist that any framework subordinating three to one will produce the characteristic pathology of the hypertrophied axis. The English vocabulary of "values" does not give this structural decomposition. "Wellbeing" tends to flatten into kama-plus-dharma-minus-artha-minus-moksha. "Purpose" tends to flatten into dharma-minus-everything-else.

The discourse of "work-life balance" gestures at the purusharthas problem (the integration of artha and kama under dharma) and has no apparatus for the moksha dimension at all. The result is a framework in which the legitimate spiritual aim of human life is treated as a hobby or private therapy - a structural omission the purusharthas frame corrects.

## Where it Shows Up

**Product values and single-axis optimization.** A product can be evaluated on each purusharthas axis. Does it serve the user's artha (resources, capability, power)? Their kama (enjoyment, beauty, connection)? Their dharma (helping them meet what their situation calls for)? Their moksha (supporting deeper freedom, or closing it off)? Most product value frameworks score on a single axis - engagement equals kama, productivity equals artha - and produce predictable pathologies in the dimensions they neglect.

**Strategic decisions and the multi-aim frame.** A company has artha aims (revenue, market position), dharma aims (obligations to users, employees, the broader system), kama aims (the satisfaction of building something), and arguably moksha aims (the deeper purpose that transcends any quarter's results). Single-axis strategy produces the pathologies the purusharthas frame predicts. The frame makes explicit which aim is being prioritized and what is being sacrificed.

**Personal life design.** The classical use. A life that pursues artha (career) at the expense of kama (relationship, pleasure), dharma (role-obligations), and moksha (deeper development) produces a characteristic pathology. The frame gives structural decomposition to what life-design vocabulary handles informally as "balance."

**AI alignment and the question of human values.** The alignment literature struggles with "what are human values?" because the answer is plural and structured. The purusharthas offer a classical decomposition. A system aligned with kama alone is recognizably bad (the engagement-maximizer). One aligned with dharma alone is recognizably bad (the paternalistic enforcer). One aligned with artha alone (the resource-extractor). Alignment to all four, held in tension, is structurally more difficult and more truthful to what human flourishing actually requires.

**Therapeutic and contemplative work.** A practice addressing only one dimension produces partial benefit and characteristic distortions. The classical Indian practice traditions are generally structured to address the four aims in an integrated way. Single-modality contemporary interventions often produce hypertrophy in one dimension while the others atrophy.

## IKS Roots

The Sanskrit term is *purusharthas* (पुरुषार्थ), from *purusha* (the human, the person) + *artha* (aim, goal) - "the aims of the human." Dharma (धर्म) derives from *dhr* (to hold, to sustain). Artha (अर्थ) from *arth* (to aim at, to mean). Kama (काम) is the broader term for desire and also the name of the god of desire in the Puranas. Moksha (मोक्ष) from *muc* (to release, to free). The classical treatises addressing each separately: the Dharmashastra literature (*Manu-smriti*, *Yajnavalkya-smriti*) for dharma; Kautilya's *Arthashastra* for artha; Vatsyayana's *Kamasutra* for kama; the Upanishads, *Brahma-sutras*, and Yoga-sutras broadly for moksha. The *Bhagavad-gita* integrates all four in its analysis of right action. The *trivarga* (dharma-artha-kama) is the householder's frame; the addition of moksha makes the *caturvarga* - the complete human aim-structure. The ashramas (life-stages) pair the four aims with the four stages of life: brahmacharya (student, dharma-formation), grihastha (householder, artha and kama within dharma), vanaprastha (forest-dweller, turning toward moksha), sanyasa (renunciate, moksha-focused). Buddhist and Jain traditions inherit and modify the analysis, generally giving moksha (nirvana; mukti) systematic priority within a similar four-fold frame.

See also Y1 (the vritti frame within which the kama and dharma dimensions of mental activity arise), Y4 (avidya - whose master operation conditions all four aims), and V1 (adhyasa - the cognitive event by which the four aims get displaced into pathological substitutes when the structural relations are missed).
