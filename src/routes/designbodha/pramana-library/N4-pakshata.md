When you infer that "this hill has fire because there is smoke on it," the inference has a structure with several components: the property to be established (fire), the reason (smoke), the relation that grounds the connection (pervasion - see N2), and the locus where the inference is being made (this hill). The locus is the *paksha*. *Pakshata* is the condition of something being a valid locus - what makes it the place where the question actually lives.

For something to be a valid paksha, the property in question must be neither already known to be present nor already known to be absent. The hill must be one where fire is an open question. If we already know fire is there, the inference is empty - nothing remains to establish. If we already know fire is absent, the inference is contradicted at the start. The paksha is the place of the live question.

This sounds obvious until you watch inquiries fail at exactly this step. A research question is posed about a locus where the answer is already settled - the inquiry produces no new knowledge because the paksha offers none. A product strategy anchors on a locus that turns out to be the wrong layer of the system entirely. A debugging session misidentifies where the problem lives. In each case, the reasoning is plausible, the question is well-formed, and the locus is mis-set. The inquiry runs but does not attach to anything.

## Where English Falls Short

The nearest English concept is framing - how a question is set up shapes the answer. Framing is psychological: how is the question presented, how does presentation bias the responder? Pakshata is structural: where, in the architecture of the inquiry, is the question anchored, and is that the right anchor for what is actually at issue? Two inquiries can have identical framing and different pakshas. Two can share a paksha and be framed entirely differently. These are distinct considerations.

The other near-cousin is scope - what is in and out of the question. Scope is about boundaries; paksha is about substrate. A scope statement says "we are looking at these things." A paksha says "this is the thing the question is actually about."

## Where it Shows Up

**Research question formulation.** "Why is engagement dropping?" - what is the paksha? The cohort? The feature? The time window? The platform? Each is a different locus, and the inquiry into "why engagement is dropping in this specific locus" is a different inquiry from "in that one." Many research projects burn cycles because the paksha was set at the wrong layer before the work began.

**Debugging.** "The system is broken." What is the paksha - the request handler, the data layer, the cache, the client? The first move in good debugging is locating the actual locus of the failure, before any hypothesis is tested. The paksha gets refined as the investigation progresses; most debugging confusion is pakshata confusion.

**Prompt design.** "Help me write better" - what is the paksha? The opening sentences? The structure? The vocabulary? The rhetorical posture? A prompt that fails to locate its own paksha leaves the model to choose one. The choice is often wrong, and the resulting output confirms it.

**Strategic decisions.** "We need to grow." Where? The user base, the revenue per user, the geographic footprint, the product surface? "Grow" without a paksha dissipates into general intention. The locus is where the question becomes operational.

**Model evaluation.** "Is this model good?" - at what? The paksha of the evaluation determines what counts as a pass. Many AI eval debates are really pakshata disagreements: each side is evaluating at a different locus and reading the same evidence to support contrary conclusions.

## IKS Roots

The Sanskrit term is *pakshata* (पक्षता), the abstract noun derived from *paksha* (पक्ष), the locus or subject of inference. The classical analysis treats paksha as one of the three terms in any inference: *sadhya* (what is to be established), *hetu* (the reason offered), and *paksha* (the locus where the sadhya is at issue). Gangesha and the Navya-Nyaya school elaborated pakshata as a technical condition - the locus is a valid paksha only when neither *siddhi* (the sadhya already established there) nor *badha* (the sadhya already ruled out there) obtains. The operational lever is the underlying structural insight: questions have substrates, and the substrate must be located before any inquiry can actually proceed.

See N2 (vyapti - the relation) and N3 (sapaksha / vipaksha - the instance sets) for the other technical components of inference.
