The Nyaya taxonomy of inferential failure has five canonical members. Most name ways an offered reason fails from within - a reason that appears where the property is absent (*anaikantika*), a reason that entails the opposite (*viruddha*), a reason not yet established (*asiddha*), a reason whose conclusion is ruled out by a stronger pramana (*badhita*). Satpratipaksha is the odd one out. It does not name a reason that fails. It names a situation where the offered reason is individually sound - and a contrary reason, equally sound, is in play for the opposite conclusion.

Both reasons survive their own tests. Neither is invalid by the standards applied to it. They point in opposite directions and cancel. The inferential machinery does not adjudicate - it stalls. The Nyaya response is principled: when a reason is counterbalanced, no inference is yielded. The locus remains epistemically open.

This is a real cognitive event, distinct from contradiction (where one side is wrong) and distinct from ordinary uncertainty (where the reasons are weak). The reasons are strong. The situation is unresolvable by the standard inferential procedure. Naming it lets you stop brute-forcing a resolution and ask a different question: what would have to be true at a deeper level for one side to gain force?

## Where English Falls Short

The nearest English construct is "competing hypotheses." But the standard Bayesian move is to compute posterior probabilities - to assume the disagreement is resolvable in principle by collecting more evidence and updating. Satpratipaksha names the situation where the two reasons are structurally at parity, where the available evidence cannot adjudicate. More evidence does not break the deadlock. The obstacle is the symmetry of the reasons themselves.

"Equipoise" in clinical-trial ethics captures part of the structure - the situation where two treatments are believed equally effective, making randomisation ethical. Equipoise is a probabilistic-belief state about a single underlying fact. Satpratipaksha is a structural feature of the reasons themselves. The distinction matters: one calls for data collection; the other calls for finding the asymmetry the current frame cannot see.

## Where it Shows Up

**AI eval debates.** Two models produce contrary outputs, each defensible on its own terms. One is more helpful but more verbose; the other is more concise but flatter. Most teams adjudicate ("which is better?") and argue past each other indefinitely. The satpratipaksha move is to recognize the counterbalance, stop forcing adjudication, and ask what deeper consideration - a stakeholder priority, a use-case context the eval did not encode - could break the symmetry.

**Policy decisions.** "Centralise vs decentralise." "Move fast vs ship safe." "Aggregate vs personalise." Most of these debates carry legitimate reasons on both sides that survive scrutiny. Forcing adjudication produces hollow consensus or factional victory. Recognizing counterbalance opens space to ask: at what locus does this question become asymmetric?

**Research synthesis with split literature.** Half the papers find the effect; half find none; both sets are methodologically sound. The reflex is a meta-analysis to declare a winner. The satpratipaksha response is to ask whether the two clusters are at parity by construction - different operationalizations of the variable, different populations - and to treat the literature as actually split until the asymmetry surfaces.

**Product strategy.** Two directions, each supported by valid arguments and valid evidence. Forcing the team to choose by debate produces noise. Naming the situation as counterbalanced reframes the decision: what would have to be true for one direction to gain decisive force, and how would we know?

**Ethical conflict in design decisions.** Privacy vs personalization, transparency vs simplicity. The classical ethical dilemma is often satpratipaksha: both sides carry real reasons that survive their own tests, and the dilemma is genuine because the reasons are counterbalanced - not because one side is confused.

## IKS Roots

The Sanskrit term is *satpratipaksha* (सत्प्रतिपक्ष), literally "a reason whose contrary reason exists." Of the five hetvabhasa-s (fallacies of the reason) at Nyaya-sutra 1.2.4 - savyabhicara (the deviating reason), viruddha (the contradictory reason), prakaranasama (early form of satpratipaksha), sadhyasama (later identified with asiddha), and kalatita (sublated by time, later identified with badhita) - satpratipaksha alone concerns counterbalance between two valid reasons. The other four name internal failures of the offered reason; satpratipaksha names a situation where the reason is internally fine but externally cancelled by an equally valid contrary reason. Gangesha's *Tattvacintamani* elaborates the technical machinery for testing it.

See N2 (vyapti - the relation a reason must ground), N3 (sapaksha / vipaksha - the instance sets used to test it), and N4 (pakshata - the locus where the inference is being made).
